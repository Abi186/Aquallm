# Water Quality Management

General guideline ranges for vannamei ponds. Actual limits can differ with salinity, pond type and farm SOP. Always follow your farm technical advisor for decisions.

## Dissolved Oxygen (DO)

DO is the oxygen available to shrimp, measured in mg/L (same as ppm). A level of 5 mg/L or higher is preferred. Below 4 mg/L shrimp are stressed and feed intake and growth drop. Below 3 mg/L is dangerous, and below 2 mg/L can cause mortality.

DO is lowest in the early morning (before sunrise) because plankton and shrimp use oxygen at night. Check DO at night and at dawn. Low DO is made worse by high stocking density, heavy feeding, high biomass, dying plankton blooms, and too few paddlewheel aerators.

If DO is low: start or increase aeration, reduce or skip the next feed, avoid sudden water exchange, and check for plankton crash.

AquaLLM alert thresholds: DO below 4 mg/L is shown as a high concern, and DO below 5 mg/L is shown as below the preferred level.

## Temperature

Vannamei grow well at about 28 to 32 degrees Celsius. Below about 25 degrees growth slows and feed intake falls. Above about 33 degrees shrimp are stressed and DO falls because warm water holds less oxygen. Sudden temperature changes (for example after heavy rain) are stressful.

AquaLLM alert thresholds: temperature outside 26 to 32 degrees Celsius is flagged.

## pH

A pH of about 7.5 to 8.5 is generally ideal. The difference between morning and evening pH should be small (less than about 0.5) because large daily swings stress shrimp. High pH in the afternoon usually means a heavy plankton bloom. Low pH can come from heavy rain, acidic soil or too much organic matter.

AquaLLM alert thresholds: pH below 7 or above 9 is flagged.

## Salinity

Vannamei tolerate a wide salinity range, and many farms run at roughly 10 to 25 ppt (parts per thousand). Sudden salinity change, for example after heavy rain, stresses shrimp more than the absolute value. Change salinity slowly.

## Ammonia

Ammonia is a toxic waste from feed and shrimp excretion. The unionized form (NH3) is the dangerous one, and it increases with higher pH and temperature. Unionized ammonia should stay as low as possible, ideally below about 0.05 to 0.1 mg/L. Rising ammonia usually means overfeeding, excess organic waste, or a weak biofloc / bacterial balance.

AquaLLM alert threshold: ammonia above 0.05 mg/L is flagged as elevated.

## Nitrite

Nitrite is also toxic. Keep nitrite low (commonly below about 1 mg/L, and lower at low salinity). High nitrite means the nitrification process is not working well, often from poor bottom condition or too much feed.

## Alkalinity and hardness

Total alkalinity of about 100 to 150 mg/L (as CaCO3) helps keep pH stable. Low alkalinity causes pH swings. Minerals such as calcium, magnesium and potassium are important for moulting and shell hardening, especially in low-salinity water.

## Transparency and plankton

A water transparency of roughly 30 to 40 cm (Secchi disk) is common in many ponds. Very clear water allows weeds and unwanted algae; very turbid or dark water means a heavy bloom with a risk of DO crash. A stable green or brown colour is generally healthier than a sudden colour change.

## Hydrogen sulphide and pond bottom

A black, smelly pond bottom produces hydrogen sulphide (H2S), which is toxic. Good aeration (with proper placement for sludge collection), controlled feeding and bottom cleaning reduce the problem.
